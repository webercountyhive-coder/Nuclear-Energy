The Federal Permits

A DOE categorical exclusion from full environmental review, a military airlift, and a new NRC rule that lets commercial licenses reuse DOE and military reviews. Here is what each federal record says, and what it says about the public.

Posted Oct. 29, 2026 · Updated Oct. 29, 2026
Read the plain-words version →

The short version

  • A new NRC rule takes effect Nov. 25, 2026. It lets a company seeking a commercial reactor license reuse reviews the Department of Energy or Department of War already did, "to the extent that those reviews cover the same scope."
  • Since Feb. 2, 2026, DOE can approve advanced test reactors without a full environmental review. It used that exclusion for Valar's reactor in Emery County. The form has no public notice or comment language.
  • Valar told the NRC in April it had no plans to apply for a license, and asked that the plan be withheld from the public. In August it filed a revised plan "outlining plans to seek NRC licensing."
  • An earlier Hive check of the 136 comments on the NRC rule found none from the State of Utah. Utah is a plaintiff, with Valar, in a lawsuit against the NRC over small reactors.

The new NRC rule, in its own words

The rule says an application "may include consideration of relevant information gathered from a utilization facility that has been previously authorized by the Department of Energy or the Department of Defense and that has been tested and has demonstrated the ability to function safely. Any reference to such a design must identify how attributes of the authorization satisfy NRC regulations ..."

The NRC says it "will not need to repeat technical reviews previously performed by DOE or DOW ... to the extent that those reviews cover the same scope." It also says "Nothing in this rule delegates to DOE, DOW, or any other agency the NRC's functions" and that it "was not able to quantify these potential savings in a useful way at this time."

Who commented

  • Valar Atomics: "We urge the Commission to finalize this proposed rule without delay."
  • New York called crediting a DOE or military authorization without independent review "an inappropriate abdication of NRC's responsibility," and asked how public participation from the earlier approvals would be carried over.
  • Uranium Watch (Moab), in a joint letter with Beyond Nuclear and others, wrote that the operator at the San Rafael lab "did not provide any information," listing "emergency response planning" among the missing items.
  • HEAL Utah: "Efficiency must never come at the expense of public health, safety, or democratic accountability."
  • Oregon Department of Energy: "The NRC cannot allow itself to be treated as a junior partner in a multi-agency effort to promote nuclear energy."
  • State of Utah: an earlier Hive check (Oct. 24–25, 2026) found no comment from the governor, the Office of Energy Development or the Department of Environmental Quality on the docket.

The NRC's full answers to the 136 comments are in document ML26282A996. We have not read it yet.

DOE's categorical exclusion for advanced reactors

DOE's exclusion B5.26 took effect the day it was published, Feb. 2, 2026. DOE then took 30 days of comment, which it called voluntary. Attorneys general from 11 states and D.C. objected on Mar. 4, writing that "DOE's process to promulgate this CE included no public participation in advance of the effective date" and that "DOE has no authority to authorize commercial power generation or industrial application." Utah did not sign.

The Ward 250 form (CX-271015) says "the proposed action is hereby categorically excluded from further NEPA review." It describes a 250-kilowatt-thermal reactor on about 2 acres of a 20.6-acre site, run for about 12 months, then 6 months of cooldown and 6 of decommissioning. It has no public notice or comment language. The Utah historic preservation office "concurs that no historic properties will be affected."

The reactor's size doesn't match from record to record. DOE's form says 250 kilowatts thermal. Valar's quality description, as reported, says 100 kilowatts "initial test power." The American Nuclear Society reported 10 kilowatts on June 22. The Air Force article calls it "a 5-megawatt nuclear reactor." DOE called the June 18 test "zero-power"; an earlier Hive page recorded the state lab's site calling it "full-power." We report them side by side.

Public hearings are changing too

  • Apr. 15, 2026: the NRC changed its mandatory hearing rule without prior public comment, citing a procedural exception in federal law.
  • June 8, 2026: mandatory hearings now come about 30 days after an application is accepted, with each member of the public allowed "no more than five minutes at a time," plus two weeks for written comments.
  • Proposed Mar. 3, 2026: "eliminate discretionary intervention" in contested hearings, with shorter windows to file. Not final as of Sept. 14.
  • Proposed July 15, 2026: "remove all instances of the term ALARA" (as low as reasonably achievable) and let licensees "apply for higher limits for members of the public at large." The NRC says the change "would impact Agreement States," which includes Utah.
  • In Congress: Sen. John Curtis cosponsors S. 1757, which would end mandatory hearings for uncontested licenses. Sen. Mike Lee sponsors S. 4284, a DOE path to authorize commercial reactors on federal land or for federal purposes.
  • NRC staffing: the Government Accountability Office says the NRC "lost, on net, about 500 staff" from July 2024 to June 2026 (GAO-26-108737).

The other side, from the same records: the NRC says the right to request a contested hearing is unchanged and the new rule does not lower its safety standard. BLM says the public will get input on Valar's land request through the federal environmental review. TerraPower's Wyoming permit and Holtec's Michigan application went through standard NRC notice and hearing steps.

The military

Hill Air Force Base appears in the record only as the stop where the Ward 250 reactor landed. The Army's nine candidate sites, its Janus program awards (Aug. 26, 2026, five bases) and the Air Force's first microreactor sites name no Utah installation.

At Hill AFB on Feb. 15, 2026, Under Secretary of War Michael Duffey said: "It's clear to me that advancing President Trump's priority on nuclear energy depends on close coordination between the Department of Energy and the Department of War." The Air Force article reports, in its own words, that he said the future of warfare is energy-intensive and includes AI data centers, and that the War Department will need to build its own energy infrastructure.
