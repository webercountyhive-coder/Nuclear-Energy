Utah's Nuclear Bills, 2023–2026

Fifteen bills and resolutions that built Utah's nuclear framework: a regulator, a fund, a campus law, and two exemptions from the state's high-level waste law.

Posted Oct. 29, 2026 · Updated Oct. 29, 2026
Read the plain-words version →

The short version

  • From 2023 to 2026, lawmakers passed bills that created a Nuclear Energy Consortium, a state nuclear regulatory office, an energy fund, and a law for hosting a federal nuclear campus.
  • H.B. 78 (2026) added two exemptions to Utah's law that otherwise requires the governor, the county and the Legislature to approve placing high-level nuclear waste in Utah.
  • One fund ties four bills together: radioactive waste taxes flow into it, and the nuclear campus law lets it be spent on the campus.
  • S.B. 135 (2026) names "data centers" in a required review of state law. Its report was due Oct. 31, 2026. We did not find it posted as of Oct. 29.

H.B. 78 (2026): the two waste-law exemptions

Utah law says no one may place high-level nuclear waste or greater-than-class-C waste in Utah unless the governor approves, after consulting the affected county, and the Legislature concurs (Utah Code 19-3-301). H.B. 78 added two exceptions.

Exemption 1, Utah Code 19-14-103(1)(b): that part of the code "does not apply to nuclear fuel cycle activities regulated or licensed by the division under this chapter." The bill defines the fuel cycle to include enrichment, fuel fabrication, reprocessing, "storage of nuclear material," and waste management.
Exemption 2, Utah Code 19-3-321: it "does not apply to interim storage of nuclear waste if" the waste comes from a project authorized by the U.S. Department of Energy, is from a test reactor, is produced at a facility owned by the state, and the project "is permitted and licensed by the division."

Those three conditions match the setup at the state-owned San Rafael Energy Lab, where Valar Atomics runs a DOE-authorized test reactor. The bill does not name it. See the lab page, including what we found (and did not find) about a state permit.

How the bill changed on the way through

  • Jan. 21, 2026: House committee added "fuel conversion" to the fuel-cycle list (vote 9-0-3).
  • Jan. 30, 2026: Sen. Karen Kwan's amendment added report items, including "any significant challenges or issues encountered in implementing this chapter, including safety measures" (Senate committee 5-0-3).
  • Feb. 26, 2026: Sen. Derrin Owens' 1st Substitute added 19-3-321, kept the 19-14-103(1)(b) exemption, removed "fuel conversion" from the definition, and removed the words ", including safety measures" from the report item. It kept "staffing levels and training activities."
  • Mar. 4–5, 2026: Senate passed the substitute 23-2-4 (no votes: Blouin, Plumb). House agreed 61-8-6.

Money: the fiscal note says $60,900 one-time and $380,000 ongoing for the new office, $440,900 from the General Fund. The note's table and text disagree on which year the $60,900 falls in.

Who spoke: in committee, Emy Lesofski of the Office of Energy Development spoke for the bill. Speaking against: Joan Entwistle and Nathan Hollen (Sierra Club), Lexi Tuddenham (HEAL Utah) and resident Abbie Eastwood. The first annual report from the new office is due Nov. 30, 2026.

S.B. 216 set a one-time tax of $3.45 per cubic yard of new licensed waste-disposal space, capped at 8.7 million cubic yards (about $30 million), plus a 16.67% tax on waste from first-time generators through June 30, 2028. A coordination clause sent that money into the fund H.B. 249 created. S.B. 135 then lets the Utah Energy Council use the same fund for the nuclear campus.

HEAL Utah wrote (Feb. 13, 2025) that S.B. 216 was "run at the request of Energy Solutions." That is an advocacy group's claim; we did not verify it.

S.B. 135 (2026): data centers named in nuclear law

The law orders the Office of Energy Development, the Nuclear Energy Consortium and the radiation division to review state law for anything that could keep Utah from hosting a federal nuclear campus. The review must cover "co-location of advanced manufacturing, data centers, or high-heat industrial processes" with nuclear generation, and "conflicts between Title 19, Chapter 3" and federal standards for advanced reactors.

By Oct. 31, 2026, the office must report its findings and "a proposed framework for creating a nuclear innovation zone." As of Oct. 29, 2026, we did not find that report posted. The Oct. 21 interim committee agenda did not list it.

The law also says the office must "engage willing communities to develop consent-based siting for campus elements."

How it changed: the original bill had the consortium run a nuclear fuel recycling program with a feasibility study and a public outreach program. The 1st Substitute removed the program, the study and the outreach. The 2nd Substitute added the campus, the review and the innovation-zone report.

What these bills do NOT say

Some claims circulate about other bills. We checked the bill texts (read as text through a reading tool). These do not contain nuclear language:

  • H.B. 546 (2026) requires mapping "priority mineral lands." The text read does not mention uranium or Bears Ears.
  • H.B. 412 (2026) requires wildlife consultation for solar and wind over 10 megawatts. It is not a nuclear exemption.
  • H.B. 222 (2026) limits liability for greenhouse gas emissions for any "person." It does not mention nuclear.
  • S.B. 57 (2024), the Constitutional Sovereignty Act, has no nuclear, energy or NRC language.

Questions to ask, and to whom

  • Ask: Your state senator and representative — Will the S.B. 135 report on a "nuclear innovation zone" be posted for the public before any bill is drafted from it?
  • Ask: Public Utilities, Energy and Technology Interim Committee (Sen. Owens, chair) — Why were the words "including safety measures" removed from H.B. 78's annual report requirement?
  • Ask: Division of Waste Management and Radiation Control — Has any project been "permitted and licensed by the division" under Utah Code 19-3-321? If so, which one, and where is the permit posted?
  • Ask: Utah Energy Council — How much is in the Electrical Energy Development Investment Fund now, and how much came from radioactive waste taxes?
